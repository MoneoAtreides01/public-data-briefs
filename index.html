01 — Energy systems

The 50% club keeps swapping members

Twenty-six national grids changed sides on majority-renewable electricity between 2000 and 2025. Sixteen joined. Ten left. The two groups barely resemble each other.

26 grids changed status 16 joined, 10 left, across 156 countries
0 wind or solar on the leavers all ten were hydro-only in 2000
5 of 7 entrants since 2020 arrived with wind or solar already built

What the data shows

  • The net number barely moved. 51 grids were majority-renewable in 2000, 57 by 2025 — a gain of six that hides twenty-six changes of side.
  • The leavers were hydro-only. Not one of the ten had a megawatt of wind or solar in 2000. Hydro output moves with rainfall and dam availability; installed wind and solar do not.
  • The joiners changed kind. None of the three grids that joined in the 2000s arrived on wind and solar. Five of the seven that joined since 2020 did.

Method & sources

Ember electricity data via Our World in Data; World Bank populations, used only to drop territories under one million people. Sample: 156 countries with a usable annual series from 2000.

“Majority renewable” means renewables supplied at least 50% of electricity generation in a given year. Classification of what tipped a country over uses its wind and solar share in the crossing year.

02 — Prices

Groceries kept up with wages. Restaurants didn't.

Food at home is up 31.9% since January 2020. Average hourly earnings are up 33.0%. Measured against pay, the grocery basket is roughly where it started. The gap opened at the restaurant.

+31.9% food at home January 2020 to September 2026
+33.0% average hourly earnings same window
+4.9 pts food away from home, vs wages the only category meaningfully ahead of pay

What the data shows

  • The spread inside one basket is wider than the headline. Fruit and vegetables rose 40.1%; “other food at home” rose 19.0%. That is a 21-point gap between aisles.
  • Nothing has come back down. Meat, poultry, fish and eggs sit 1.0% below its peak. Every other category is at its highest level since 2020.
  • Eating out breaks the pattern. Food away from home is up 37.9%, nearly five points ahead of pay, while groceries finished just behind it.

Method & sources

BLS Consumer Price Index (CPI-U, seasonally adjusted) and CES average hourly earnings, both via FRED. January 2020 to September 2026, the latest month available.

Wages are an economy-wide average, not a household measure. Regional prices, basket composition and median income all differ from these national series, and a household that buys more fresh produce than the average basket has felt a different number.

03 — Technology cost

Solar's price curve held for 25 years, with one four-year detour

Each doubling of installed solar capacity has come with a 27.7% fall in module price. One slope and one intercept explain 96% of the variation. The exception is 2008–11, when a polysilicon shortage pushed prices as much as 72% above the curve.

27.7% price fall per doubling fitted on 25 annual observations, 2000–2024
0.96 R² of the fit one slope, one intercept, no other inputs
96% fall in module price $6.50/W in 2000 to $0.27/W in 2024

What the data shows

  • The curve is a planning tool, not a law. Prices in 2008–11 sat 45–72% above the fitted line, then reverted within three years.
  • Solar is the outlier among technologies. Its levelised cost fell 89% between 2010 and 2025. Onshore wind fell 71%. Hydropower rose 41% over the same period.
  • The rate is a forecast you can use. At 27.7% per doubling, another doubling of installed capacity implies roughly a quarter off the module price.

Method & sources

Solar module cost, installed capacity and levelised cost of energy from IRENA and Lazard via Our World in Data. The experience curve is fitted by ordinary least squares on log price against log cumulative installed capacity.

Module price is one component of system cost; installation, land and financing are excluded and have fallen more slowly. The 2008–11 residual is a known polysilicon supply shock, not a fitting error.
